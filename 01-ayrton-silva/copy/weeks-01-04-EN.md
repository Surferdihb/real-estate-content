## W1 · Thursday — Market read: Esch-sur-Alzette

**Hook**
In Esch-sur-Alzette, the gap between what sellers ask and what actually gets
paid is €950 per square metre.

**Body**
Average asking price: €7,149/m².
Average notarial price, per the Observatoire de l'Habitat: roughly €6,200/m².

That gap isn't an anomaly. It's the single most useful fact about the Esch
market, and almost nobody puts it on the table.

What it means in practice: on an apartment listed at €600,000, there is a
structural negotiating margin that has nothing to do with how good a negotiator
you are. It's in the market itself.

The rest of the picture, July 2026:
— Asking prices: −1.31% year on year
— The market has corrected 5.4% from its January 2026 peak (€7,556/m²)
— Over 3 years: −6.3%
— Since 2019: **+47.1%**

That last line is the one people forget. The post-2022 correction is real, but
the decade's gain is largely intact. Anyone who bought in 2019 is fine. Anyone
who bought in 2022 is waiting.

Meanwhile rents are climbing: +3.22% year on year, to €28.17/m²/month.
Prices down, rents up. For an investor that's the right direction — though as
I'll show next week, it still isn't enough.

**Context for non-residents:** Luxembourg publishes notarial prices quarterly
and with a lag, at commune level. Asking prices you can see live on athome.lu
and immotop.lu. Everything else is guesswork dressed up as data.

## W2 · Tuesday — The neighbourhood nobody looks at

**Hook**
Within Esch-sur-Alzette there's a 1.75× spread between the most and least
expensive neighbourhood. And the best yield isn't where you'd expect.

**Body**
By zone, sale and rent:

Grenz — €7,480/m², rent €14.28 → 2.29% gross yield
Fettmeth — €6,971/m², rent €18.07 → 3.11%
**Al-Esch — €6,580/m², rent €27.70 → 5.05%**
Park — €6,439/m², rent €16.80 → 3.13%
Belval-Université — €4,911/m², rent €15.75 → 3.85%
Dellhéicht — €4,284/m², rent €14.25 → 3.99%

Al-Esch is the outlier. It's the only neighbourhood where prices are still
rising (+1.1%) *and* where rent is double everywhere else (€27.70/m² against
€14–18). The historic centre. The only part of Esch where gross yield
approaches 5%.

And the reverse: **Belval-Université underperforms.** That's counterintuitive —
the university, the new-build stock, the station. But at €4,911/m² against rent
of €15.75/m², it sells well below the commune average and rents poorly. Prices
flat at −0.0%. A lot was built there, and student rent has a ceiling.

The general lesson: in Esch the address matters more than the property. The same
apartment can double its yield depending on the street.

## W2 · Thursday — An analysis that says no

**Hook**
I analysed a €675,000 apartment in Esch. Score: 54 out of 100. Here's why I
don't recommend it as a rental investment — and why it's still a good buy as a
primary residence.

**Body**
The property: 103.59 m², 2 bedrooms plus office, 2nd floor, cellar. €6,516/m².

What's good:
— 9% below the commune's average asking price (€7,149/m²)
— Comfortable size, renovation underway
— As a primary residence: Bëllegen Akt tax credit, resale capital gain exempt,
  low energy costs

What's wrong, and it's decisive if you're buying to let:
— Cash-flow **negative by roughly €940 per month** at 80% LTV
— **DSCR of 0.62** — for reference, a bank wants to see above 1.0
— Gross yield 3.68%

Put plainly: every month you put €940 of your own money in to hold this asset.
For years. That's a bet on capital value, not a yield investment. It can be
defended — but you need to know it going in and have provisioned for it, not
discover it in month three.

The same property as a primary residence is a considerably stronger case. The
property is good. It's the **investor case** that doesn't hold.

That's what I mean by honest analysis. A grade C doesn't mean "bad property."
It means "good property, wrong use."

**Two terms for non-residents:**
*Bëllegen Akt* — a tax credit that can wipe out up to €40,000 of registration
duty for owner-occupiers. Doesn't apply to buy-to-let.
*DSCR* — debt service coverage ratio. Below 1.0 means the rent doesn't cover the
mortgage.

## W4 · Tuesday — What Luxembourg doesn't publish

**Hook**
Four numbers every American buyer gets for free, and which simply do not exist
in Luxembourg.

**Body**
1. Sale-to-list price ratio — **impossible**. No public register of transaction
   prices, no MLS.
2. Share of properties selling above asking — **impossible**, same reason.
3. Average days on market — **not published**. Not by the Observatoire, not
   STATEC, not the Chambre Immobilière.
4. Months of inventory by commune — **not published**. Transactions are only
   released at national and cantonal level.

I spell this out because I see a lot of analyses quoting these metrics to two
decimal places. They're invented. You cannot compute them here — the underlying
data doesn't exist.

What we actually have is two sources of different natures:
— **asking prices** (athome, immotop) — what sellers want
— **aggregated notarial prices** from the Observatoire — what gets paid,
  published quarterly, with a lag

And the gap between them is itself the information.

An honest analyst tells you what they don't know. It's how you spot one.

**Why this matters if you're buying from abroad:** the due diligence habits you
bring from the US, UK or France won't transfer. The data layer is thinner here.
Budget more time for local comparables and less faith in dashboards.

## W4 · Thursday — Niederkorn: the real cost of buying

**Hook**
A €545,000 apartment costs you €588,600. Here are the €43,600 nobody mentions
before signing.

**Body**
The full calculation, on a reference property in Niederkorn (Differdange
commune):

Price: €545,000
Registration and transcription duty (7%): **€38,150**
Notary (~1%): **€5,450**
**Capital actually invested: €588,600**

That €588,600 is what every yield calculation should be based on. Not the
€545,000. This is the most common error I see in investment files — including
ones prepared by professionals: yield gets calculated on purchase price, which
mechanically overstates it.

On this file, the difference between the two methods moves cash-flow from −€940
to −€984 per month. Sounds small. Over twenty years it's €10,560.

Two more things worth knowing:
— The **Bëllegen Akt** can erase up to €40,000 of duty for an owner-occupier.
  It does not apply to buy-to-let.
— The duty reform (bill 8184) **has not passed**. Until it does, you calculate
  at 7%. I've seen simulations circulating with the reformed rate already
  applied — that's premature.

A note on this specific file: no active listing was found at this address. The
analysis therefore models a reference property at the observed Niederkorn price
level. It's a location and market analysis, not a valuation of a specific
property. I flag it because the distinction matters.
